THE GATES OF MORNING

CHAPTER I

THE CANOE BUILDER

Standing on a coral ledge, Dick looked out toward the south.

Behind him, the breakers of the outer sea thundered, flinging their spindrift on the wind; before him stretched Karolin’s lagoon, calm as a lake, vast and blue, circled by wheeling gulls.

Enclosed by its forty-mile ring of coral, this immense expanse was a sea unto itself—storm-tossed in heavy gales, an azure mirror under gentle breezes—and it was his, though he had landed here only yesterday.

Women, children, and youths—the entire tribe scattered along the sun-drenched beach, netting fish, playing games, or tending the paraka patches—all were his people. His, too, were the outriggers hauled high upon the sand, and the empty sheds where the great war canoes had once rested upon their rollers.

Then, as his gaze shifted from the lagoon to the canoe houses, his brow furrowed. Turning his back on the sheltered water, he faced the breakers of the outer reef and the northern sea. Far out beyond the horizon, unseen, lay Palm Tree—an island lovely as a dream, yet crawling with devils.

Sitting nearby on the coral, little Tari, son of the net-maker Le Taioi, watched him. Tari knew little of the wider world, but he understood that war had carried off all the fighting men of Karolin, leaving the women, the boys, and children like himself defenseless, without a chief to guide or protect them.

Then yesterday, out of the northern sea in an unfamiliar boat, this stranger had arrived alongside Katafa, the girl swept away by a gale while fishing years before. The women had proclaimed him sent by the gods to be their ruler.

The child knew nothing of the gods, nor did he care. Finding himself alone with this wondrous newcomer, safely out of his mother’s hearing, he asked with all the frank simplicity of childhood:

“Taori,” said little Tari, “who are you? *É kamina tai?*”

Even if Dick could have answered, could the child have grasped the strange tale he might have told? *“Tari, I come from a people beyond the world you know. My name is Dick Lestrange, and when I was smaller than you, I was left alone with an old sailor on that island you call Marua—Palm Tree—which lies out of sight fifty miles to the north. There we lived, and there I grew. Kearney, that was his name, taught me to fish and cast a spear, and he made me things to play with—little ships unlike the canoes of the islands. And then, Tari, one day long ago came Katafa, the girl who was blown away from here in a storm. She lived with us until Kearney died, and then we two were alone. She taught me her language, which is the language of Karolin. She named me Taori; we loved one another and might have lived forever at Marua had not a great ship come, filled with bad men from the eastern islands of Melanesia. They came to cut the trees. But they rose up, killed the white men with them, and burned the ship. In our little boat we escaped, taking with us everything we loved, even the toy ships, and steering for Karolin, we came, led by the lagoon light glowing in the sky.”*

Yet he could not tell Tari this—not in full—for the very name of Dick had vanished from his memory, along with the tongue he had spoken as a boy. Kearney, the old sailor who had raised him, was almost forgotten now, half-lost in the bright haze of the past.

For those long cast away in solitude, yesterday blurs and softens; for Dick, memory truly began only when Katafa reached Marua. Everything before that seemed swallowed by the searing tropical glare that drenched sea and shore, lost in the storms that battered the coconut groves and the mists of the rainy seasons. Kearney would have been forgotten entirely were it not for the miniature ships he had carved as playthings for the boy who had now grown into a man.

Looking down at the questioning child, Dick answered, “I am Taori, little Tari. Why do you ask?”

“I do not know,” said the boy. “I ask as I breathe, but no big folk—no *madyana*—will ever answer the questions of Tari— Ai! The fish!” His fickle mind instantly dropped the subject at the shouts of children hauling in a net; scrambling to his feet, he trotted away down the sand.

Dick turned his eyes northward once more. The child’s question had stirred the embers of memory: he saw again the schooner putting in at Palm Tree only to be burned by its mutinous crew; he saw Katafa and himself fleeing in the old dinghy Kearney had taught him to handle as a child; and he saw their arrival on this beach yesterday, surrounded by swarming women and children who hailed him as a leader sent by the gods.

As he stared north, the thought of the men from whom they had fled poisoned the beauty of sea and sky.

There was no immediate danger from the mutineers who held Palm Tree, for they had no canoes; yet they would build them—surely they would build them. Just as surely, they would catch sight of the distant lagoon light reflected against the clouds above Karolin, just as he had, and they would come. It might be a long time yet, but they would come.

Dick was nearly a native—a *kanaka*, living the life of an islander—and yet the white man’s mind endured within him. He possessed the gift of foresight: he could turn a problem over in his thoughts and anticipate what lay waiting in the shadows.

That was why he had dispatched a canoe that morning to the southern shore for Aioma, Palia, and Tafata—three elders past the age of battle, but master craftsmen. That was why, surveying the gathered tribe, his eyes had brightened at the sight of nearly a hundred youths approaching fighting age. Yet beneath all his plans, firing his spirit and lifting his courage to eagle heights, was his devotion to Katafa—his other self, dearer to him than life, now shadowed by a lingering peril.

War canoes! Did he intend to fight the invaders within the lagoon, meet them at the outer reef, or strike first, destroying the threat at its source before it could gather strength? Who could say?

A light hand rested on his shoulder. Turning, he found himself face to face with Katafa. A strand of dark hair, slipping free from the vine that bound it, streamed back on the breeze like an eagle’s feather; her luminous eyes looked past his own, fixed upon the very point he had been watching—the northern horizon.

“Look!” she whispered.

At rare intervals and under certain conditions of the atmosphere, Palm Tree—though sunken far below the curve of the ocean—became visible from Karolin through a mirage. They had glimpsed it the evening before, and now once more the vision began to stir, to bloom, to flicker into life: a faint blur low against the horizon, a dull spot in the ocean’s glare that deepened and sharpened until, as though sketched by an unseen hand, the island hung before them—lovely as a dream, diaphanous yet vivid.

With her hand resting upon his shoulder, the two stood watching in silence. Simple and untutored, knowing nothing of the science of the mirage, they stared at the distant refuge from which they had fled, now rising so strangely above the sea as if returning their gaze.

In memory, they saw once more the horde of savages along the beach, monstrous as figures in a nightmare; they felt again the wind filling the sail as the dinghy raced for the open sea; and they heard anew the shrieks of the Melanesians, maddened by rum plundered from the torched schooner. There before them hung the theater of that terror, an idyllic vision hiding the devils who roamed its shores.

As Dick gazed upon this loveliness, which was both a threat and an omen, his nostrils flared and his eyes darkened with hatred. They had threatened him—that was nothing; they had threatened Katafa, which was everything—and the threat remained.

Someday they would come. The specter of Palm Tree seemed to confirm what instinct told him. They would fashion canoes, follow the bright reflection of Karolin’s lagoon upon the clouds, and cross the sea. Those men had no women, while here women lived; instinct warned him that just as he had been drawn to Katafa, so would those savages hunger for the women of Karolin. They would scan the horizon for an island to raid, and guided by that pale sky-glow, they would descend.

Yet had he known, danger lay not only to the north, but wherever greed, lust, or hatred might roam upon that azure sea—not only among island savages, but among the wolves of civilization.

To Dick, there was no world beyond the water that ringed the two islands; no Europe, no America, no history save his own brief life and the life of Katafa. Yet even in that narrow compass, he had learned to dread his fellow men, grasping by instinct the grim foundation of all human history: the appetite for war, rapine, and ruin.

Gradually the vision of Palm Tree dissolved, vanishing like a blown-out flame. As they turned from the open sea back toward the sheltered lagoon, Katafa pointed across the water to an outrigger approaching from the southern shore.

It was the craft Dick had sent to fetch the canoe-builders. Leaving the coral ledge, they walked together down to the white sand of the inner beach to meet it.
