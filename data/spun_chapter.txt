"The Gates of Morning"

CHAPTER I

THE CANOE BUILDER

Standing on a coral ledge, Dick looked out toward the south.

Behind him, the breakers of the outer sea thundered, scattering their spindrift on the wind; before him stretched Karolin’s lagoon, calm as a lake, vast and blue, circled by fishing gulls.

Enclosed by its forty-mile ring of coral, this immense expanse was a sea unto itself—storm-tossed in heavy gales, an azure mirror in gentle breezes—and it was his, though he had landed here only yesterday.

Women, children, and youths—the entire tribe scattered along the sun-drenched beach, netting fish, playing games, or tending the paraka patches—all were his people. His, too, were the canoes hauled up onto the sand, and the empty sheds where the war canoes had once rested upon their rollers.

Then, as his gaze shifted from the lagoon to the canoe houses, his brow furrowed. Turning his back on the sheltered water, he faced the breakers of the outer beach and the northern sea. Far out beyond the horizon, unseen, lay Palm Tree—an island lovely as a dream, yet crawling with devils.

Sitting nearby on the coral, little Tari, son of the net-maker Le Taioi, watched him. Tari knew little of the wider world, but he understood that war had carried off all the men of Karolin, leaving the women, the boys, and children like himself defenseless, without a leader to guide or protect them.

Then yesterday, out of the northern sea in an unfamiliar boat, this newcomer had arrived alongside Katafa, the girl swept away by a gale while fishing years before. The women proclaimed him sent by the gods to be their chief and ruler.

The child knew nothing of the gods, nor did he care. Finding himself alone with this wondrous stranger, safely out of his mother’s hearing, he asked directly, with all the simplicity of a child:

“Taori,” said little Tari, “who are you?” (é kamina tai)

Even if Dick could have answered, could the child have grasped the strange tale he might have told? “Tari, I come from a people beyond the world you know. My name is Dick Lestrange, and when I was smaller than you, Tari, I was left alone with an old sailor on that island you call Marua—Palm Tree—which lies out of sight fifty miles to the north. There we lived, and there I grew to be a boy. Kearney, that was his name, taught me to fish and use a spear, and he made me things to play with, little ships unlike the canoes of the islands. And then, Tari, one day long ago came Katafa, the girl who was blown away from here in a storm. She lived with us until Kearney died, and then we two were alone. She taught me her language, which is the language of Karolin. She named me Taori; we loved one another and might have lived forever at Marua had not a great ship come there filled with bad men, men from the eastern islands of Melanesia. They came to cut the trees. Then they rose and killed the white men with them and burned the ship, and in our boat we escaped from them, taking with us everything we loved, even the little ships, and steering for Karolin, we came, led by the lagoon light in the sky.”

Yet he could not tell Tari this—at least not all of it—for the very name of Dick had vanished from his memory, along with the tongue he had spoken as a child. Kearney, the sailor who had raised him, was almost forgotten now, half-lost in the bright haze of his past.

For men long cast away in solitude, the past blurs and softens; for Dick, memory truly began only when Katafa reached Marua. Everything before that seemed swallowed by the searing tropical glare that drenched sea and shore, lost in the storms that battered the coconut groves and the mists of the rainy seasons. Kearney would have been forgotten entirely were it not for the little ships he had carved as playthings for the boy who had now grown into a man.

Looking down at the questioning child, he answered, “I am Taori, Tari tatu, why do you ask?”

“I do not know,” said the child. “I ask as I breathe, but no big folk—madyana—will ever answer the questions of Tari— Ai, the fish!” His fickle mind instantly dropped the subject at the shouts of children dragging in a net; scrambling to his feet, he trotted away.

Dick turned his eyes northward once more. The boy’s question had stirred his memories: he saw again the schooner putting in at Palm Tree only to be burned by the Melanesian crew; he saw Katafa and himself escaping in the old dinghy Kearney had taught him to handle as a boy; and he saw their arrival on this beach yesterday, surrounded by swarming women and children who hailed him as a leader sent by the gods to be their chief.

As he stared north, the memory of the men from whom he had fled with the girl tainted the beauty of sea and sky.

There was no immediate danger from the men who had seized Palm Tree, for they had no canoes; yet they would build them—surely they would build canoes. Just as surely, they would catch sight of the distant lagoon light reflected against the clouds above Karolin, just as he had, and they would come. It might be a very long time yet, but they would come.

Dick was nearly a native—a kanaka, living like a savage—and yet the white man endured within him. He could look ahead, turn a problem over in his mind, and foresee what lay in wait.

That was why he had dispatched a canoe that morning to the southern beach for Aioma, Palia, and Tafata—three elders past the age of battle, but master canoe-builders. That was why, when surveying the gathered tribe, his eyes had brightened to see nearly a hundred youths approaching fighting age. Yet beneath it all, firing his spirit and lifting his courage to eagle heights, lay his devotion to Katafa—his other self, dearer to him than his own life, now shadowed by a vague yet lingering peril.

War canoes! Did he intend to fight the invaders within the lagoon, meet them as they neared the shore, or strike first, destroying the danger at its source before it could gather strength? Who knows?

A hand fell upon his shoulder. Turning, he found himself face to face with Katafa. A strand of dark hair, slipping free from the thread of elastic vine that bound it, streamed back on the breeze like an eagle’s feather; her luminous, dark eyes looked past his own, fixed upon the very point he had been watching—the northern horizon.

“Look!” said Katafa.

At rare intervals and under certain conditions of weather, Palm Tree—though sunken far below the rim of the ocean—became visible from Karolin through mirage. They had glimpsed it the evening before, and now once more it began to stir, to bloom, to flicker into life: a faint blur low down against the horizon, a dull spot in the sea’s glare that deepened and sharpened until, as though sketched by an unseen hand, the island hung before them, lovely as a dream, diaphanous yet vivid.

With her hand upon his shoulder, they watched in silence. Simple and untutored, understanding nothing of mirage, they stared at the distant refuge from which they had fled, now rising so strangely above the sea as if returning their gaze.

In memory, they saw once more the horde of savages along the shore, figures monstrous as shapes in a nightmare; they felt again the wind filling the sail as the dinghy raced for safety and the open sea; and they heard anew the shrieks of the Melanesians, maddened by rum stolen from the schooner they had brought in and torched. There before them lay the scene of that tragedy, an idyllic vision showing nothing of the demons who still inhabited it.

As Dick gazed upon this loveliness, which was both a threat and an omen, his nostrils flared and his eyes darkened with hatred. They had threatened him—that was nothing; they had threatened Katafa, which was everything—and they threatened her still.

Someday they would come. The specter of Palm Tree seemed to echo what instinct told him. They would build canoes, follow the bright reflection of Karolin’s lagoon glowing on the sky, and come. Those men had no women, while here women lived; instinct warned him that just as he had been drawn to Katafa, so would those men be drawn to the women of Karolin. They would scan the horizon for an island to raid, and guided by that lagoon light, they would come.

Ah, had he known, danger lay not only to the north, but wherever greed, desire, or hatred might roam upon that azure sea—not only among savages, but with the wolves of civilization.

To Dick, there was no world beyond the water that ringed the two islands; no Europe, no America, no history save his own brief life and the life of Katafa. Yet even in that short span, he had learned to dread his fellow men, grasping the grim foundation of all human history: the instinct for war, rapine, and ruin.

Gradually the vision of Palm Tree began to fade, vanishing at last like a light blown out. As they turned from the open sea back toward the lagoon, Katafa pointed across the water to a canoe approaching from the southern shore.

It was the craft Dick had sent to fetch the canoe-builders. Leaving the coral ledge, they walked down to the white sand of the inner beach to meet it.
